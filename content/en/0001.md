The word "model" has three common meanings. The first refers to a person — someone who poses for a painter or sculptor, or a fashion model. The second is a physical replica, like a plastic model kit, an architectural scale model, or a model train. The third meaning is "something that serves as a standard or template" — which is how the word is used in "AI model." This post is about that third meaning.

In this context, a "model" is a mathematical representation of something complex in the real world. A weather model, for example, represents the movement of the atmosphere as equations and runs calculations on a computer. An economic model uses assumptions about human behavior to build equations. AI models follow the same idea: they learn from large amounts of data to build a set of mathematical relationships.

What makes an AI model different is that humans don't write the rules directly. Instead, the model looks at large amounts of data and finds the patterns in it. These patterns might be something like "this word tends to follow that word." The model keeps learning pattern after pattern. Of course, it's still humans who design how the model learns.

ChatGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), and Gemini (Google) are examples of what's called a "large language model," or LLM. They've been trained on vast amounts of text from the internet, which lets them answer questions and write text. Because they learn from language — words and sentences — they're called "language models." Other well-known language models include Llama (Meta), Grok (xAI), DeepSeek, and Mistral (Mistral AI).

What a model can do depends on what kind of data it was trained on. "Generative AI" refers to AI models that create something new — text, images, and so on. There are also models designed to recognize or understand things.

Among generative AI models, "image generation models" have drawn attention over copyright questions involving anime artwork. These models are trained on large numbers of images paired with their descriptions. You type in text, and the model generates a matching image — for example, "a cat sleeping on a beach at sunset." Examples include DALL-E 3 (OpenAI), Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion (Stability AI).

"Image recognition models" work in the opposite direction. Like image generation models, they're trained on images paired with descriptions — but instead of turning text into images, they take an image as input and describe what's in it as text. Examples include GPT-4o (OpenAI) and Claude (Anthropic).

Some models, like Gemini (Google), can do both — generating images and recognizing them.

"Speech recognition models" are trained on large amounts of audio paired with transcriptions, which lets them convert spoken words into text. A well-known example is Whisper (OpenAI), trained on a large collection of audio from the internet along with its transcriptions.

Other models trained on medical data can detect cancer in X-ray images. Med-PaLM 2 (Google) was accurate enough to answer questions at the level of the US medical licensing exam. Models trained on legal documents can retrieve past case law instantly or analyze the risks in a contract. Harvey is already in use at major law firms.

**Origin of the name:**

Anthropic's AI, Claude, is said to be named after the 20th-century mathematician Claude Shannon, though Anthropic hasn't officially confirmed this. Shannon founded the field of "information theory," which established a mathematical framework for how information can be measured and transmitted accurately. That framework became the foundation for modern AI models. The core mechanism of LLMs — representing large amounts of data as equations and predicting the next word — builds on the ideas Shannon pioneered.
